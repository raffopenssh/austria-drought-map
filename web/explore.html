Risk Calculation

The drought risk score combines four factors:

  • Groundwater trend (35%): Linear regression on annual mean water table levels. Negative trends (declining water table) increase risk.
  • Hydropower impact (25%): Weighted impact of nearby plants within 30km radius (see below).
  • EDO Combined Drought Index (25%): Satellite-derived drought indicator from Copernicus, integrating precipitation, soil moisture, and vegetation stress (2012-2023 average).
  • Soil permeability (15%): Low-permeability soils reduce groundwater recharge (INSPIRE Austria soil data).

River flow trends and precipitation trends are shown as separate informational layers and in the municipality detail view, but do not enter the composite score directly (they overlap with EDO CDI and groundwater signals).

Hydropower Impact Factor

The hydro_factor calculation considers:

  • Proximity: Plants within 30km radius, with distance decay (closer = more impact)
  • Plant type weights:
    • Storage (Speicherkraftwerk): 0.7 - major sediment trapping
    • Pumped storage (Pumpspeicherkraftwerk): 0.5 - moderate impact
    • Run-of-river (Laufkraftwerk): 0.3 - lower sediment impact
  • Sediment trends: Declining sediment transport indicates upstream dam trapping, used as modifier
  • Normalized to 500 MW weighted impact = 100%

Precip vs GW Divergence Layer

  • Per station (3,727 eHYD monthly stations): z-scored 12-month rolling precipitation sum from nearest NASA POWER cell vs monthly groundwater level; best correlation lag searched 0–24 months.
  • Median r = 0.34, median lag 0 months. Divergence = mean over last 5 years of (GW z-score − lagged precip z-score).
  • Finding: median divergence −0.55σ; ~70% of stations sit below where precipitation alone would put them — consistent with abstraction and structural losses, not just meteorological drought.
  • Caveats: 0.5° precipitation grid is coarse in Alpine terrain; z-score comparison assumes roughly linear precip→recharge response.

Hydropower ↔ Groundwater Coupling Layer

Question: how much of day-to-day groundwater variation moves with hydropower operations (pumping/turbining), beyond what rainfall explains?

  • Data: 227 live eHYD groundwater stations (daily levels, current year, ~190 days) × 292 live eHYD river gauges (daily stage) × ENTSO-E Austrian generation (15-min → daily GWh) for run-of-river, reservoir, and pumped storage (generation and pumping consumption) × NASA POWER daily precipitation.
  • Method: per-station OLS on daily first differences (ΔGW). Baseline model: precipitation (today, lags 1–3 days, 7-day sum) plus a live river gauge's Δstage (today + yesterday), where the gauge is matched along the river network (Dijkstra on 338k OSM/Geofabrik waterway segments; ~85% of stations matched along-network, median ~10 km along-river, straight-line nearest as fallback) — this control absorbs the shared rain/snowmelt → river → bank-connected-aquifer pathway. Full model adds 6 power terms (each hydro class, today + yesterday). Reported value is the excess partial R²: variance uniquely added by the power terms, minus the k/(n−p−1) inflation expected from adding 6 regressors by chance.
  • Placebo check: rerunning with the power series shifted back 100 days (keeps weekday/autocorrelation structure, breaks real-time coupling) yields a median excess of ~0.4% — versus ~6% for the real series — so the measured association is not a statistical artifact.
  • Result (2026): precipitation alone explains a median 13% of daily ΔGW; adding the local river gauge raises the baseline to 27%; power terms add a median ~6% excess on top. Stations within 5 km of a hydro plant show ~9% median excess vs ~5% beyond 5 km — a plausible proximity gradient.
  • Interpretation — read carefully:
    • ENTSO-E data is a national aggregate; no plant-level attribution is possible.
    • The gauge control is matched along the OSM waterway network (not straight-line), but a single gauge still cannot capture the full river system; residual shared hydrology may remain in the power signal.
    • Austrian pumped storage is mostly high-alpine, (semi-)closed-loop — direct aquifer coupling is physically expected to be near zero; any statistical association is likely weekday/price seasonality shared with other variables.
    • Bottom line for the risk model: we find no evidence that power-plant pumping is a first-order driver of groundwater decline at national scale; the dominant unexplained signal remains the negative precip-vs-GW divergence (abstraction/structural). Hydropower affects groundwater mainly indirectly — via impoundment, sediment trapping and altered river–aquifer exchange — which is what the hydro_factor (25%) already represents.

Sediment Transport Analysis

  • 34 sediment monitoring stations analyzed
  • Trend calculated: recent 3-year average vs. historical 3-year average
  • Declining trends may indicate upstream dam sediment trapping
  • Example findings:
    • Enns at Steyr: -64% decline
    • Inn at Schärding: 12,674 t/day average
    • Salzach at Ach: +98% increase

Precipitation Trend Analysis

  • 861 precipitation stations analyzed (daily data since 1971)
  • Annual totals calculated (minimum 300 days/year required)
  • Linear trend over 10+ years, outliers removed
  • Risk factor: -100 mm/decade = 100% risk, +100 mm/decade = 0% risk
  • Distance-weighted average from stations within 30km
  • Finding: 231/861 stations show declining precipitation
  • Example trends:
    • Zürs-Hexenboden: -449 mm/decade (Alpine decline)
    • Pfänder: +313 mm/decade (Lake Constance increase)
    • Mean trend: +9 mm/decade

Surface Water Flow Analysis

  • 604 flow gauging stations analyzed (Q-Tagesmittel)
  • Annual mean discharge calculated (min 300 days/year)
  • Linear trend over 10+ years
  • Risk factor: -20%/decade = 100% risk, +20%/decade = 0% risk
  • Links to hydropower: identifies MW capacity on same rivers
  • Finding: 390/604 stations show declining flow (mean: -2.8%/decade)
  • Example trends:
    • Traisen at Herzogenburg: -67% (severe decline)
    • Rußbach at Leopoldsdorf: +42% (increasing)

Groundwater Trend Analysis

  • Minimum 10 years of data required
  • Outliers filtered (trends > 5m/decade excluded)
  • Stations with unrealistic variance excluded
  • Result: 94 stations with valid trends
  • Finding: 54 declining, 40 rising (mean: -4.7cm/decade)

Limitations

  • Coordinate transformation (BMN to WGS84) is approximate
  • Risk interpolated to municipalities without direct monitoring
  • Hydropower impact is proximity-based, not fully hydrologically modeled
  • Sediment stations matched to municipalities via river names (no direct coordinates)
  • River connectivity not modeled (upstream/downstream relationships)
